Dish proof

Three dishes,
and why they taste the way they do.

Dum Biryani

The rice is not supposed to look the same all the way through.

A biryani is layered and finished on dum, sealed and cooked slowly in its own steam, the mark of proper Indian cuisine in Lyon. Different grains carry different amounts of aroma, spice and colour, and that unevenness is the point. If every grain matches, something has been stirred that should not have been.

Old Delhi Butter Chicken

The target is a balance that exists in Old Delhi.

Not a generic restaurant butter chicken adjusted out of a mother gravy. The reference is the version cooked where the dish came from, and the work is getting the tomato, the fenugreek, the smoke and the butter to sit in the same proportion here as they do there.

Mangalore Fish Curry

You have to eat it in Mangalore before you can cook it in Lyon.

Fresh mango, coconut milk, curry leaves and a Mangalore spice blend, held in a balance that is regional rather than general, is what regional Indian food in Lyon should taste like. The sourcing matters as much as the method, and the only way to know the target is to have tasted it on the coast.

How we cook

No shortcuts,
because the shortcut is the difference.

Specialist suppliers

Six to eight specialist suppliers, chosen per ingredient rather than per invoice.

Spice blends made in-house

The masalas are built here, not bought in. Each dish has its own blend, not a shared base.

Layered and slow

Layered cooking and long slow-cooking wherever the dish demands it. Nihari and biryani are not fast food.

The right vessels

Handis and lagans sourced from Chawri Bazaar in Delhi. Heat retention and cooking behaviour are part of the result.

Spiced, not necessarily hot. Chilli heat is reduced by default. Chilli achars at different heat levels sit on the table, so you add fire without changing the dish itself.

Before it was a restaurant

It was a research question.

53weeks, 53 regional menus

Deb’s master’s thesis studied how French diners perceive and accept Indian food, starting with the pre-perception created by familiar curry-restaurant codes. He then ran fifty-three weeks of fifty-three different regional Indian menus and compared what diners made of the food afterwards.

A paturi wrapped in banana leaf could be read as tropical or island food. Butter chicken was instantly coded as Indian. That gap became the logic behind Deb’s Bistro, the Indian restaurant in Lyon built to broaden the understanding of Indian food without making the food less Indian.
